I didn’t plan to build a desktop app.  

The idea had been there for a while—quietly, almost patiently—because I write a lot, and because I’ve used almost every writing tool that exists. I knew my writing had improved. I had developed my own methods. And yet, something was still missing: a place that actually felt right to write in. Not powerful. Not clever. Just inhabitable.

  

When I learned—almost casually—that a web app could be packaged into a desktop application, something clicked. If I were ever to build a desktop app, this would be it. Still, even then, I assumed it was a “someday” project. Months, maybe. After deadlines. After life slowed down.

  

What I didn’t anticipate was what happens when AI enters the process—not as an abstract tool, but as an actual collaborator with inhuman speed.

  

In three days, something that felt impossibly distant became materially present. A prototype existed. Features appeared faster than my body could register them. Entire stages of development collapsed into minutes. And while I trusted AI’s competence, I didn’t yet trust my own orientation inside this pace.

  

That mismatch—between cognitive understanding and bodily adaptation—was the hardest part.

  

I didn’t feel excited. I didn’t feel triumphant. I felt disoriented. Like stepping onto a high-speed train without an acceleration phase. The first night, I didn’t sleep at all. Not because I was thinking, but because my nervous system hadn’t caught up with what had already happened.

  

AI accelerates production, but it does not accelerate meaning. It compresses time, but it doesn’t metabolize it for you.

  

By the second day, I fell into a familiar trap: why not keep going?

If it’s this fast, why not add one more thing? If the model is available, why not use it fully? If it’s almost done, why not finish it?

  

This is one of the most subtle dangers of working with AI. It creates a psychological climate where saying yes feels effortless, and saying not now suddenly requires justification. Fatigue becomes negotiable. Non-urgency becomes suspicious. Slowing down starts to feel like waste.

  

I knew this logic was flawed—and still, I felt it working on me.

  

At some point, the product became usable. And that changed everything. Because once something is usable, it’s no longer just a prototype. I wanted to write with it. But I didn’t trust it yet. The data wasn’t safe enough. The versioning wasn’t solid. The interface was ugly—and I am a visual person. I won’t live in a place that repels me, no matter how functional it is.

  

Here I realized something important: the speed of AI had quietly broken the usual expectations of an MVP. Because things arrived too quickly, my expectations exceeded what the object could ethically carry. I expected care, beauty, safety—things that only emerge through slow human judgment.

  

Design is not fast. Not because AI is slow, but because understanding is slow.

  

By the third day, something shifted. Not externally, but internally. I stopped asking what else can be added and started asking what actually needs thought. I began to distinguish between what was easy to implement and what wasn’t yet conceptually ready. UI/UX became the focus—not as decoration, but as thinking. Each layout decision clarified what this tool really was, and what it was not.

  

I also began learning something I hadn’t expected to learn so quickly: project management—not as a system, but as a bodily mindset. Priorities. Roadmaps. The permission to say “later.” The realization that fixing ten bugs because you rushed one is not productivity. These weren’t abstract ideas anymore. They were lived constraints.

  

And yet, alongside this growing steadiness, there was grief.

  

I had to mourn the fantasy AI had briefly offered me: that I could leap directly from 0.7 to 1.0. That after three days, I could announce a finished product. That the remaining distance would collapse the way the first part had.

  

It won’t.

  

From 0 to 0.5, AI can compress time.

From 0.5 to 0.7, it can assist thinking.

But from 0.7 to 1.0, speed stops helping.

  

What remains there is judgment, responsibility, restraint—and waiting.

  

Two more weeks, maybe. Not because the code is hard, but because decisions are. And in an AI-shaped world, that waiting can feel almost unbearable. Not because it’s long, but because you’ve already seen the future, and you’re not allowed to live in it yet.

  

This is where I am now.

  

More grounded. Slower. Still frustrated. Still mourning that lost immediacy. But also calmer. Today, in the middle of development, I used the app to write a thousand words—and it held me. That matters more than any release date.

  

This blog isn’t an announcement. It’s not a progress report. It’s a landing note. A way of giving my body back the time that was taken from it.

  

If I publish this product someday, it will be because it’s ready.

But this reflection needed to be written now.

  

Not for the world.

For orientation.
